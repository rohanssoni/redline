## What to build
A flag's wording hedges ("this could allow…", "this may be read as…") **only when
its source sentence is itself genuinely open to more than one reading** — so every
hedge the reader sees is something they can check by rereading that sentence.

Hedging is never used for any other uncertainty: not how a court would rule, not
real-world consequences, not a general low-confidence score. Those produce a
**plainly-stated flag**, or the clause is dropped by the plausibility filter.

This requires flag generation to carry **textual ambiguity** and **harm confidence
as two separate signals**, not one score. How that distinction is produced —
separate fields in the structured output, a second classification pass, prompting
alone — is your call. The spec deliberately leaves it open. **Record the choice and
its reason** in a short ADR under `docs/adr/` (next free number, matching the style
of the existing ones) and say so in your report.

## The fixtures give you both cases
In `tests/fixtures/adhesion-agreement.json`:
- `decoys.textuallyAmbiguous` — the flag `ambiguous-standards-update-right`, the
  only one with `textualAmbiguity: true`. Its sentence has a real attachment
  ambiguity. This one may hedge.
- `decoys.nonTextualLowConfidence` — the flag `overbroad-non-compete`, plainly
  worded, `harmConfidence: "partial"`, uncertain only as to enforceability. This
  one must **never** hedge.

## Tests
- A fixture built around genuine sentence-level ambiguity yields hedged wording.
- A fixture built around a different kind of low confidence yields plain wording
  or no flag, **never** hedged wording.
- Assert on the produced wording, not on an internal boolean alone — the hedge is
  a thing the reader reads.
